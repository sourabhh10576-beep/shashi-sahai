He photographed what happened between things.

Shashi had a quality that is increasingly rare: he knew how to make another human being feel important. He gave affection without measuring status, age, gender or social position.

Children responded to him naturally. Friends relied upon him. People carrying difficulties often found themselves speaking to him because he could listen without making their vulnerability smaller.For him, photography was less about the camera and more about paying attention. Ironically, the man who spent decades documenting others rarely wanted attention himself. Shashi remained intensely private and understated throughout his life. He was uncomfortable with self-promotion and rarely spoke about his own achievements. Those close to him say he would much rather hear another person’s story than tell his own.

For much of his life, Shashi stood behind the lens. It was where he was most comfortable watching, listening and waiting for the moment that others often missed. A Photojournalist by profession a photographer by instinct, had a long association with the media and contributed to several publications, including international magazines. Colleagues remember him as a keen observer with a rare ability to find a story in the seemingly ordinary.

A story of friendship

The quiet way he stayed beside us.

For nearly twenty years, we shared work, purpose and the small routines that slowly become a life together. Shashi never needed to announce his support. When a difficult decision or a long day arrived, his steady presence was already there.

As cofounder and a long-standing pillar of Aarohan NGO located at Malviya Nagar, New Delhi, he worked for years in support of children, women and vulnerable communities. His contribution went far beyond designation.

He became one of the people the organization could depend upon, someone who worked quietly, solved problems and remained present when he was needed. He did not believe every act of kindness required an audience. Much of what he did for others was done without photographs, announcements or recognition. Perhaps that was the clearest reflection of the man he was. He could meet a stranger and, before long, make that person feel like an old friend irrespective of age, profession or social standing. People entered his life as acquaintances and often stayed as family.

But the final months of Sahai’s life were very different from the years he had spent moving through the world with a camera in hand.

Yet cancer is only the final paragraph of his story. It is not the story itself.

That story lies in the decades that came before it. It is in the photographs he left behind and the stories he helped tell. It is in Aarohan and the communities he served. It is in the people he helped when nobody was watching. It is in friendships that outlived assignments, workplaces and distances.

For nearly twenty years, we lived and worked alongside each other. We shared a commitment to causes larger than ourselves. Through Aarohan and beyond it, Shashi gave his time, energy, experience and affection without keeping an account of what he gave or what he received. There were no fixed hours for him when somebody needed help. Resources could be scarce, circumstances could be difficult, but these things rarely altered the purpose we shared. We could disagree, but our differences never became distance. There was an extraordinary freedom in our relationship. Neither time, money, position nor circumstance could determine its strength.

For me, his support was something I once described as an afternoon shadow—sometimes you may not notice it, but it remains beside you. Whether I was dealing with a difficult decision, an organisational crisis, a personal worry or simply the exhaustion of the day, I knew Shashi was there. Sometimes that assurance required no conversation at all. He was also a puzzle. Even those who knew him for years probably knew only parts of him. He seldom spoke about his own agony. He could carry hurt quietly and still find the emotional space to comfort somebody else. Perhaps that was one of his contradictions: a man who concealed his own wounds but was extraordinarily sensitive to the wounds of others. Our relationship never needed a name. At different moments he could be my closest friend, my critic, my guide, my mentor, my companion in a cause, and the person before whom I could put down the burdens I carried. We did not spend our years defining the relationship; we spent them living it.
